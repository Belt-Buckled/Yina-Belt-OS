Days Like These

The name is chosen. This document does the part that matters more: it takes the four places the name lost in the head-to-head and turns each one into a rule, so the weaknesses are handled by system instead of by hope.

The lockup
Days Like These
Favors, decor and custom apparel. Made by hand, one at a time.
Made for days like these.
Why this name

It holds two eras in three words.

You say days like these about a memory. You also say it about a day you are living right now. That is contemporary nostalgia without ever using the word, and it is the same doubleness that shows up in the one real customer: a concert shirt for an artist she loved young, and a birthday shirt for her toddler, from the same hands.

It also covers apparel, favors, decor and digital files without naming any of them, which means the business can widen without a rename. Renaming after you have customers is expensive.

The bet being made, stated plainly: 100% of revenue so far is apparel and 0% is favors. This name was chosen for the business described in the plan, not the business proven on the receipts. If the kids' party this quarter produces no favor sales, Grown & Growing was the better answer.

The work

Four losses, four rules.

Left side is where the name failed. Right side is what makes it stop failing.

Loss 1 · The kids' birthday listing

Brand-first titles made an upcoming party sound wistful
The problem
Days Like These — Custom Birthday Shirt, 5T

Leading with the brand points a nostalgic phrase at a party that has not happened yet. It reads like a memorial instead of an invitation.

The rule — product first, brand last
Custom Birthday Shirt, 5T, Any Name | Days Like These

Nobody is searching for the brand yet. Put what they typed first and sign it at the end. The tense fix does the rest: made for days like these points forward, at the day coming up.

Loss 2 · Said out loud at a party

Three common words, easy to forget, hard to search later
The problem
"She has a shop, it's called Days Like These." — "...Days like what?"

Word of mouth in a mom circle is currently the whole distribution channel, so the sentence a customer repeats matters more than any ad.

The rule — the handoff line
"It's called Days Like These. She makes custom shirts and party stuff."

Fifteen words. Name plus category, no jargon. Say it this way every single time so it gets repeated this way. Recovery line when someone mishears: "Days Like These, like the phrase."

Loss 3 · The Instagram bio

The name explained nothing in the first two seconds
The problem
Days Like These

A mom scrolling gives you two seconds. A bare abstract name spends both of them without telling her anything.

The rule — the lockup
Days Like These
Favors, decor and custom apparel.
Made by hand, one at a time.
DM to order.

The name never appears bare where a stranger might meet it. The descriptor always travels with it. Bare name is allowed only where the audience already knows her: a hang tag on a delivered order, a repeat customer's DM, a reply in her own comments.

Loss 4 · The cold stranger

Could be a candle shop, a podcast, a photographer, a coffee roaster
The problem
Days Like These — ?

The quarter's entire test is whether a stranger buys. Being unreadable to a stranger is the most expensive weakness on the list.

The rule — collections are occasions
Days Like These · Birthdays

Organize the catalog by the day, not the product. The name stops being abstract the moment it is followed by an occasion, and a stranger instantly knows what this is.

Structure

Collections are occasions, not product types.

This is the structural fix. A birthday collection holds a shirt, a favor bag, a banner and a digital file, which is exactly the point: one day, every surface.

"Wait, what was I doing?"

A real, observed pattern in the demographic: start one task, get sidetracked, end up on a third thing, then backtrack to check whether the first one ever got finished. The audience already recognizes it, because the algorithm serves it to them daily.

It reaches two buyers at once. The mom living it, and her teenage or twenty-something kid who recognizes it in her and buys gifts for her.

Where it belongs: captions, hooks and a recurring series. The half-finished project, the five open tabs, the thing found in a bag three weeks later, then the finished piece. Not on a hang tag. Under a logo on a keepsake it undercuts the care the brand is selling.

Rejected and retired: "A ridiculous amount of thought."

Never stack two taglines in one place.

Guardrails

What this brand never says.

  • Not "merch." Merch is bought. This is made.
  • Not "print on demand." A machine did not decide anything here.
  • Not "cute." Confirmed banned. Cute is small. Milestones are not small.
  • Not kids-only. The same buyer buys for her kid and for herself. Copy that speaks only to toddler parties cuts half the customer.
  • Not reselling. Out of scope, permanently.
  • Not an artist's logo. The brand evokes an era. It does not reproduce one's trademarks.

Deliberately not decided yet: colors, typefaces, logo. The colors and type on this page are document styling, not brand identity. Visual identity comes after the words are settled.

Still open: no USPTO clearance has been run. Search class 25 (apparel) and 16 / 28 (paper and party goods) at tmsearch.uspto.gov before printing anything permanent.
